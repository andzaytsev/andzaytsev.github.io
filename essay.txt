Startups in Silicon Valley, while being a cradle of innovation, often ignore the questions of ethics in favor of making money. This alarming trend[1] can be seen across the industry, with a recent example being ReservationHop. The service makes restaurant reservations using fake names to later sell them to legitimate customers for profit. I will argue that the actions of this company are unethical from the deontological perspective.

Deontological, or the duty-based ethical system, is based on the principle of treating other people the way one would want to be treated. In the case of ReservationHop, the moral agents most affected are dining customers and restaurants. A customer may call a restaurant to discover no reservations available for the following month. Going online, they see all availabilities are booked by ReservationHop and sold for twelve dollars each[1]. Often, that amount is equal to an entire meal for a person. On the other hand, an owner of a restaurant may have tables for the next entire month booked out. While this may seem like a sign of blooming business, as the month goes on, nearly every reservation gets canceled four hours[3] in advance. Since other customers are not aware of available reservations, few tables are occupied. Low profits force the restaurant to shut down. Brian Mayer, the founder of ReservationHop, would certainly not want to be in either of those positions, which makes it unethical in relation to both restaurant customers and owners from the deontological perspective.

The topic of hurting restaurant customers and owners was brought up in several articles[1,4], implying an appeal to the duty-based moral system. As a result of such publicity, Brian Mayer made several statements about his startup, containing logical fallacies and misconceptions about ethics discussed in the class. For instance: “We shouldn't let our attitudes about what is or isn't considered taboo to decide what markets we create, as long as the markets involve mutually consensual transactions.”[5] This is a combination of naturalistic fallacy (inferring the way the markets are organized defines the way they should be) and a misconception that having a mutually consensual transaction guarantees an act is ethical. Mayer also brings up the misconception that ethics are unimportant when creating a product: "Is this even legal? Is it ethical? I haven't spent a lot of time thinking through these questions."[1] Some of the positive feedback ReservationHop received claims the startup democratizes the dining experience[2]. One article appeals to the utilitarian moral system and argues that by selling reservations, the startup makes exclusive restaurants more available for the majority of people lacking unique social connections. While that may sound good in theory, the author admits that such a system would favor the rich and makes a naturalistic fallacy and false analogy: "We pay prices for lots of other things in life, so this is one beneficial change we simply ought to get used to." While there is some positive feedback, it does not change the unethical impact of ReservationHop, especially from the duty-based perspective.

This controversy surrounding the startup points to several flaws in the current legal system. There is a policy vacuum in the space of public services such as restaurant reservations, which are intended to be free for anyone. This allows startups such as ReservationHop to charge a price for them without breaking any explicit laws[4]. The reason for this vacuum is a conceptual muddle. With the appearance of such companies, the line between ethical and unethical use of courtesy services is blurred. Now companies could make a reservation with the intention of selling it later or canceling, in which case it becomes a complex question ethically and legally. As a result, there is an urgent need for regulation similar to ticket scalping laws to prevent harm to free public services.

ReservationHop's unethical actions and the policy vacuum in the area makes it vital to see what we can do as computer professionals. Startups like this negatively reflect on the computer industry as a whole: "This is irresponsible and sleazy and exactly what people hate about startups sucking the life out of San Francisco."[1] One of the main reasons is that some startups get involved in unethical businesses for the sole purpose of making money. Thus, to preserve ethics in the industry, computer professionals should work to eliminate the policy vacuum and create some code of conduct for startups. Ethics are vital in the modern world and should never be ignored.
