Deciding whether someone is a good candidate is nontrivial. It is not uncommon to search for red flags on websites such as Facebook and Twitter. While using this public information is almost expected, a British startup Score Assured takes this data collection to a whole new level by requiring an applicant to give up the access to all of their social media accounts. I will argue that this startup is unethical from a consequence-based perspective.

Utilitarian, or consequence-based ethical system, is based on the principle of maximizing total happiness of all moral agents. Parties involved in the case of Score Assured include the startup's employees, prospective tenants, and landlords. Of all those, prospective tenants are negatively affected the most. By requiring applicants to give Score Assured full access to their social media accounts, the startup aims to gather nonpublic personal information (NPI) in addition to public personal information (PPI). The compiled report includes plenty of personal identifying information (PII) such as profile pictures[1]. As a result, many prospective tenants would feel their privacy is deeply violated. In addition, landlords and other undisclosed third parties get the ability to judge a person based on their look, religious and political views expressed in private, and individual words taken out of context. Thus prospective tenants may experience discrimination, stalking, and unwanted intimate advances. In extreme cases, the applicants may even become victims of fraud or identity theft due to their NPI turning into PPI. As a result, the overall happiness substantially decreases.

While tenants are affected the most, landlords may also experience repercussions. Gaining access to sensitive data, they may get prosecuted in jurisdictions such as the U.S., which prohibit discrimination of tenants based on age and pregnancy[1]. As Steve Thornhill, co-founder of Score Assured, put it, "All we can do is give them the information. It’s up to landlords to do the right thing." A landlord signs up for Score Assured thinking it would simplify their tenant screening, only to find themselves with lawsuits and damaged reputation. As neither of those are direct consequence of their actions, they would be substantially unhappy.

While it may be clear Score Assured leads to an overall decrease of happiness, it is important to consider counterarguments to that. Thornhill argues that "If you’re living a normal life, then you have nothing to worry about."[1] This is a straw man argument, since he ignores the expressed concerns and makes a general statement without diving into specifics. In addition, Score Assured shifts the question of morality to landlords, who are supposed to "do the right thing."[1] This is a common moral misconception of not wanting to deal with ethics. While the startup may offer some insights for the landlords, the benefit of that pales in comparison with the harm it causes to the parties involved.

To further analyze ethical consequences of Score Assured, one needs to understand the nature of the underlying data. Since the user is prompted to give their social media login information, the data is not descriptively private. It is normatively private, however, since it is not socially acceptable for third parties to obtain such deeply personal information without the user having any other choice. Thus, Score Assured violates both the norms of appropriateness and the norms of distribution. The former are violated since a background check website is not expected to drill into one's personal life. The latter is violated since the applicant is not informed who their data is shared with, in addition to not being able to even obtain a copy[1]. An additional concern is the form of data mining utilized. It takes words such as "poor" out of context and presents them to a potential landlord. This has an immense potential for misrepresentation of the facts, with the applicant being at a disadvantage. Overall, the way Score Assured treats data is questionable at best.

Establishing Score Assured is unethical in its idea and methods of dealing with data shows a policy vacuum in the area of background checks. While there is an existing U.S. law prohibiting tenant discrimination based on age and pregnancy, the startup manages to avoid legal hurdles. This situation arises due to a conceptual muddle caused by the emergence of online social media and its pervasiveness. As seen here, informed consent and industry self-regulation still put tenants at a disadvantage, as refusing to share their private data would likely result in a denial. Thus, there is an urgent need in legislation that prohibits any third parties providing housing or employment from obliging applicants to disclose online information they would be uncomfortable sharing. Score Assured showed us the problem, and it is up to us to act to uphold the ideals of morality.
